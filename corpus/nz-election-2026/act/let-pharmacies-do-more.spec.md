# What the page states

> GP access delays leave pharmacists underused despite successful minor-conditions pilots. The policy widens conditions pharmacists can treat, allows monitoring of stable chronic disease, pilots skin-lesion checks, removes pharmacy ownership caps, and enables 24-hour prescription lockers. No funding or rollout timetable is given.

System: Community pharmacy scope and access

Scenario: A patient seeks treatment for a common condition or stable chronic disease
Given one in four adults says the time taken to get a GP appointment was a barrier to seeing a GP
And New Zealand has more than a thousand community pharmacies staffed by qualified health professionals whose skills are not fully used
And more than 120,000 people used the Minor Health Conditions Service pilot in 2023, with 88 per cent having their needs met at a pharmacy
When pharmacist treatment and monitoring scope is widened with clear rules for when a patient needs a doctor
Then pharmacies may treat chest infections, ear infections, skin infections, acute pain, sore throats, tonsillitis, school sores, styes, gout, osteoarthritis, vaginal thrush and period pain, and provide STI self-swab testing
Output accredited pharmacists monitoring stable hypertension, type 2 diabetes and high cholesterol and adjusting medication within GP-set limits
And pharmacists ordering appropriate blood tests when requested or clinically necessary with results in the shared health record
And a pilot of pharmacy-based skin-lesion checks using dermatoscopes and an approved AI risk-assessment tool with referral pathways
And removal of the five-pharmacy ownership cap and proximity restrictions while cutting licensing red tape
And secure 24-hour prescription lockers for eligible medicines
Outcome GPs retain overall clinical responsibility and visibility for pharmacist-managed chronic conditions
And patients collect medicines when it suits them without joining GP queues for conditions pharmacists can safely treat
